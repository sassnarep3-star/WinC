How to assess cleanup and counter-space trade-offs

The appliance you keep using is the one that is easy to wash and easy to park. Both are checkable before you buy, from documents you can open in a minute.

Crisp Report desk·Published 20 November 2026 ·Reviewed 20 November 2026 ·5 min

Written from listings and manuals as documents, not from a kitchen. Nothing here was tested; the aim is to show which fields carry weight.

Ask anyone whose appliance sits under a cupboard, unused, what went wrong. Almost nobody says 'the crispiness was disappointing'. They say it was a faff to clean, and there was nowhere to keep it. Those two facts decide long-term use more than any performance claim on a spec sheet, and both can be checked before you spend anything.

Count the parts before you count the features

Find the exploded parts diagram - it is usually in the manual PDF or a gallery image. Then count, on one run, what touches food and what touches grease. A drawer unit is often two pieces. An oven-style unit is commonly a basket, one or two racks, a tray, a door with glass on both sides, and an interior surface you can only reach with a cloth. Each piece is a minute; the door is the one people stop doing.

The five questions that predict whether you will still use it in month eight:

  • How many pieces touch food, and are they all shown as removable?
  • Does the interior need wiping, and can you see the surface in a photo to judge it?
  • Is the coating described as a coating, or only as 'non-stick' with no type given?
  • Are replacements sold for the coating, the basket and the door seal - and does the brand's own support page list them?
  • Does the manual say 'dishwasher safe' for the part, or for the coating? Those are different promises.

'Dishwasher safe' is the most quietly overloaded phrase on an appliance listing. Sometimes it means the basket, sometimes it means the basket without the coating surviving the detergent, and sometimes it appears in the marketing image but not in the manual's care section. Read the manual's wording. If the two disagree, the manual is the document a warranty claim will be argued from.

Grease: the question listings avoid

Look for three things. Whether there is a grease catch or drip tray and how visible it is to remove. Whether the exhaust points up, back or sideways - which decides what gets greasy on your shelf. And whether any text on the page tells you what to do about the inside of the chamber after a fatty cook. A listing that describes crisping beautifully and never mentions grease has optimised for the return it hopes you will not make.

Counter space is a volume problem, not a footprint problem

Most buyers measure the width of the gap and stop. The three numbers that actually matter are on the same line of the spec table:

Body width × depthWhether it physically sits in the slotProduct dimensions table - not the carton size
Body heightWhether the drawer can open under a shelf, and whether you can see the display standing upSame table; then crouch to the height you will cook at and imagine it

Cord length and which side it exitsWhether the socket you have is usable at allGallery close-up or manual; rarely in the headline specs

Five fields, no review needed, and every one of them has caused a return.

The 'put it away' test

Decide now, before you buy, which of two households you are: the appliance lives on the counter permanently, or it lives in a cupboard. If it lives in a cupboard, the questions change completely - weight, whether the cord tucks, whether parts stack, whether it is awkward to carry one-handed. If it lives on the counter, the questions are grease on the shelf above, noise while people are at the table, and whether the finish shows fingerprints next to your kettle.

There is no correct answer, and that is the point of writing it down first. A purchase that fits the household beats a better appliance that does not.

What we will not pretend to know

  • How loud any model is in a room with a dining table. A decibel figure without its measurement conditions is not comparable between listings.

A short procedure

Open the listing. Open the manual. In one column, write the parts that touch food. In the second, write the clearance the manual asks for. In the third, write what you do when you get home: measure the slot, look up at the shelf, and check which side the cord exits. Two of the three columns are about your kitchen, which is why this is worth more than any summary sentence anyone could write for you here.
